# Teacher Guide

## Who this is for

Children ages 4–6 with no piano experience. No note-reading is assumed.
Reading is not required: every chart uses colors, letters, and pictures.
Lessons are designed for one child at a piano, but every activity has a
group variation.

## Outcomes

By the end of 12 lessons the child can:

- sit with a relaxed, curved hand shape and play with a gentle touch;
- keep a steady beat and clap quarter and eighth notes;
- find C, D, E, F, G, and A by color and by letter;
- play a repertoire of simple melodies from a color chart, left to right
  (cuckoo echo, *Hot Cross Buns*, *Mary Had a Little Lamb*, *Twinkle*
  phrases, *Ode to Joy*, *Jingle Bells*);
- recognize colored noteheads on a five-line staff;
- play a short melody with a simple left-hand drone;
- perform one chosen piece for a listener.

## Materials

**Essential**

- Piano or digital keyboard with at least 61 keys and touch sensitivity.
- This curriculum printed in color (see `printables/piano-printables.html`).
- Colored dot stickers that match the palette (or the printed key strip).
- A soft ball, two rhythm sticks or spoons, and a small stuffed animal.
- Crayons in the seven palette colors.

**Nice to have**

- Boomwhackers, desk bells, or a colored xylophone (same palette).
- A lap whiteboard and chunky markers.
- Sticker box for the progress chart.
- A phone or tablet for recording the child's playing.

## Set-up checklist

1. Bench height: feet flat on the floor or on a cushion, knees slightly below
   hip level. Forearms roughly level with the keys.
2. Distance: elbows slightly in front of the body, not tucked behind.
3. Lighting: music stand lit, no glare on the keys.
4. Volume: start at half. Digital pianos: touch sensitivity on, reverb off.
5. Remove distractions from the top of the piano. Keep one toy on the bench
   if it helps the child settle.

## The color system

| Note | Color | Hex | Shape marker |
|---|---|---|---|
| C | Red | `#D32F2F` | circle |
| D | Orange | `#F57C00` | triangle |
| E | Yellow | `#FBC02D` | star |
| F | Green | `#388E3C` | square |
| G | Blue | `#1976D2` | diamond |
| A | Purple | `#7B1FA2` | heart |
| B | Pink | `#C2185B` | hexagon |

Colors match common Boomwhacker sets. The shape markers make the system
usable for colorblind children: say "red circle" rather than only "red."

Stick one dot on the front edge of each white key from C to B in the middle
of the keyboard. Do not label every octave at first; one "home octave" keeps
the map small.

## Lesson anatomy (30 minutes)

| Minutes | Segment | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 | Hello song and settle | Routine, connection, attention |
| 3–7 | Body and finger warm-up | Posture, gross motor, finger independence |
| 7–12 | Review game | Retrieve last week's skill with movement |
| 12–19 | New concept | One idea, taught through sound and play |
| 19–26 | Piano play | Chart, echo, or rote piece at the keys |
| 26–28 | Create or perform | Improvise, compose, or play for the teacher |
| 28–30 | Wrap-up and home card | Name one success, explain practice |

Each plan uses this shape. If a child is absorbed in one activity, cut the
review game, not the piano play.

## Teaching principles

1. **Sound before symbol.** Sing or clap the tune before showing the chart.
2. **One new thing.** Each lesson introduces one concept. Everything else is
   review.
3. **Echo first.** Teacher plays, child copies. Then read the chart.
4. **Short turns, many turns.** Two minutes at the piano, then a movement
   game, then back. Young attention spans are served by variety, not by
   longer drills.
5. **Success is the curriculum.** End every lesson with something the child
   can already play well.
6. **Name the behavior, not the child.** "Your wrist is floating like a
   balloon — let's land it" instead of "you're doing it wrong."

## Posture and hand shape

- Sit tall on the front half of the bench.
- Feet supported; do not let legs dangle.
- Shoulders down, elbows relaxed.
- Curved fingers, as if holding a small bubble under the palm.
- Thumb on its side, not flat.
- Play with the fingertip pad, not a locked joint.

Teach the bubble with a real object: place a small pom-pom under the palm,
play a five-finger pattern, keep the pom-pom from escaping.

## Rhythm language

Use words, not fractions.

- Quarter note = **walk** (one step per note).
- Two eighth notes = **run-ning** (two quick steps).
- Quarter rest = **shh** (hands on knees).
- Half note = **hold** (two beats, arms open wide).

Count aloud as "1, 2, 3, 4" and clap. Only after the body knows the rhythm do
we point at the notation.

## Finger numbers

Right hand in C position: 1 thumb on C, 2 on D, 3 on E, 4 on F, 5 on G.
Left hand in C position: 5 pinky on C, 4 on D, 3 on E, 2 on F, 1 on G.

Put a small sticker with the finger number on the child's fingernails for the
first weeks. Remove it before the child leaves.

## Assessment

Assessment is informal and continuous. Use the "I can" statements at the end
of each lesson plan. Mark them on `progress-tracker.md` as:

- **Got it** — independent and secure.
- **Almost** — needs a prompt.
- **Not yet** — revisit next lesson.

Do not test note names or rhythms in writing. The child demonstrates at the
keyboard or with clapping.

## Adapting the lessons

**Very short attention (4-year-olds).** Split a 30-minute lesson into two
15-minute sessions in the same week. Keep the same order.

**Group or sibling lesson.** Run the echo games in a circle. Give each child a
turn at the piano while the others clap the beat. Color cards can be held up
by the waiting children.

**Colorblind child.** Use the shape markers and print the letter-only charts
(pages 6–7 of the printables). Say the letter name with the color every time.
Never rely on color alone for an instruction.

**Child who reads.** Let them read the letter names aloud as they play. Add
the plain-note charts earlier.

**Child who is anxious.** Keep the stuffed animal on the bench; let the child
"teach" the animal the song. Praise effort and specific actions, not talent.

**Child who races ahead.** Add the left-hand drone early, or transpose a song
to G by moving the color stickers. Do not skip the singing and clapping; they
build the inner ear.

## Common problems

| Problem | Try this |
|---|---|
| Bangs the keys | "Play as if the keys are sleeping." Practice dropping a bean bag softly onto the floor. |
| Flat fingers | Pom-pom under the palm; play with fingertips on the lid of a closed piano. |
| Loses the beat | March and clap at the same time; slow the song to half speed. |
| Looks at hands, not chart | Point to the next color with a finger while the child plays. |
| Forgets a song at home | Send a 20-second phone video of the teacher playing it. |
| Cries or shuts down | Stop the activity, do a movement break, return with an echo game the child already knows. |

## Home practice

Five minutes a day, five days a week, is plenty. Practice should feel like
playing. The `parent-guide.md` explains how to run it and what to do when the
child resists.

## After lesson 12

Move to a beginner method book that uses letter names, keeping the color
stickers available as a reference. Continue 5-minute daily practice and add
one new short piece every two weeks. The next technical goals are:

- reading steps and skips on the staff without color;
- hands together in parallel motion;
- a five-finger scale in C, G, and F;
- simple dynamics (p, f) and articulation (legato, staccato).
